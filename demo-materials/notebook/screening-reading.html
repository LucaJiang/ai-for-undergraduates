Reading: Why Prevalence Changes What a Positive Test Means

A diagnostic test does not operate in a vacuum. The meaning of a positive result depends not only on the test's sensitivity and specificity, but also on how common the disease is in the tested population.

Start with the population before the test

The disease prevalence represents the probability of disease before observing the test result. In a low-prevalence population, there may be many more people without disease than with disease. Even a modest false-positive rate can therefore produce a substantial number of false-positive results.

Why false positives can dominate

Imagine a test with 90% specificity. A 90% specificity means that 10% of people without disease will test positive. If the non-diseased group is much larger than the diseased group, that 10% can still be a large number of people. This is why a highly sensitive test does not automatically imply that most positive tests are true positives.

The direction of the conditional probability matters

Sensitivity asks: if a person has the disease, how often will the test be positive? PPV asks: if a person has a positive test, how often does the person actually have the disease? These questions reverse the conditioning event and generally have different answers.

Practical implication

Screening programs often apply a first test to people with relatively low pre-test probability, then use confirmatory testing for positive results. Interpreting a result therefore requires attention to the population being tested, the test characteristics, and the testing strategy.
