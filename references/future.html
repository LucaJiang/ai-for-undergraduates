Competition and research mathematics

IMO, July 2025

Google DeepMind's report on the advanced Gemini Deep Think system: five problems solved, 35/42 points, and a gold-medal-standard result certified by IMO graders.

First Proof Second Batch, June 2026

The benchmark authors' report evaluates four AI systems on ten research-level problems. Across all four systems combined, seven problems received at least one passing evaluation. Thirty expert mathematicians participated in evaluation; each submitted solution was assessed by at least two referees. Passing included essentially flawless solutions and those requiring minor revisions.

The report also describes a novel approach to one stochastic partial differential equation problem. These results motivate the discussion about learning alongside more capable AI. The slide asking about AI becoming better than every mathematician is a future hypothetical.

Three questions for discussion

1 · What do successful AI tasks share?

DeepSeek-R1 technical report, Section 2.2.2 uses rule-based rewards for mathematics with checkable final answers and programming tasks with predefined tests. This illustrates why clear goals, useful feedback and repeated attempts can support training.

This is one useful pattern, not a complete list of AI capabilities. A test suite also checks only what its tests cover.

Discuss: What is different about solving a given problem and choosing a problem worth investigating?

2 · Can we access and run the same system?

First Proof Second Batch, eligibility rules and Table 5 tested public models or programs calling public models. Reported costs across the ten problems ranged from about US$117 to US$4,799 across different systems. These were experiment run costs, not subscription fees.

Google’s personal-account access guide describes different usage limits and thinking levels; at this check, Deep Think is listed for AI Ultra. Work and school accounts have their own access arrangements.

Discuss: Beyond a model login, what time, budget, tools or guidance would you need?

3 · Could skill gaps shrink while wealth gaps grow?

Observed workplace evidence. Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond, Generative AI at Work studied 5,172 customer-support agents. Less experienced and lower-skilled workers saw larger improvements. This provides an example of AI helping beginners in a particular task.

Access conditions. The ILO study Disruption without dividend? examines how connectivity and differences in job tasks affect who can benefit from generative AI.

Economic mechanisms. Cazzaniga et al., IMF Staff Discussion Note (2024) analyse how complementarity with high-income workers and increased capital returns can widen income or wealth gaps. Broad productivity gains can also raise incomes. These are mechanisms and scenarios, not a settled forecast.

Discuss: If everyone received the same AI account, what differences would remain? Which kinds of support could help?

Closing reflection: one thing to understand deeply; one task to do better with AI; one resource or kind of support needed.
